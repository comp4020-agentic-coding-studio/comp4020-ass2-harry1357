## Four things a status effect can rewrite

Games sort status effects into buffs and debuffs, which tells you the sign and
nothing else. Sort them instead by which rule they edit.

**Rate** — how fast the fight moves. Poison drains HP every turn whether you act
or not. The designer didn't add damage; they added a deadline, and every plan
you had is now priced against it.

**Order** — who goes when. Paralysis halves Speed, which re-sorts a whole
Pokémon round. Slow in Final Fantasy X doubles the counter reset, which you
already know how to price from week 2.

**Cost** — what an action costs to take. Silence doesn't reduce your magic, it
makes spells unbuyable at any price. Pokémon's Torment forbids repeating a move,
which prices repetition rather than the move.

**Outcome** — what an action produces once taken. Burn halves Attack. Confusion
sometimes resolves your chosen action as a hit on yourself.

## The boxes earn their keep where they strain

Paralysis does two at once: it halves Speed, and it gives a 25% chance the turn
is simply lost. Order and outcome, in one status. That isn't a flaw in the
sorting — it's the explanation for why paralysis feels so much heavier than
"halves Speed" sounds. Anything that edits two rules costs the player two kinds
of planning, and the cost compounds rather than adds.

Now try sleep, and watch the boxes fail. Sleep doesn't change the rate, the
cost, the order or the outcome. It takes you out of the system for a few turns.
You aren't playing a modified game; you aren't playing.

That's worth sitting with, because the effects players complain hardest about
are usually the ones that turn out not to be rule changes at all. A rule change
is something you can play against. Removal isn't, and a designer reaching for it
is usually reaching for difficulty they couldn't get any other way.

## The design question the boxes let you ask

Once an effect is in a box you can ask the only question that matters: does this
fight want that rule edited? A poison in a fight that was already a race adds
nothing — the deadline was there. A poison in a long attrition fight changes it
completely. Same effect, same numbers, two different designs, and you can tell
which is which before you playtest anything.

## Lab: sort twelve effects into four boxes

Pick twelve status effects from two games, six each, with at least four that do
no damage. Put each one in a box and write a single sentence of justification.
Then find the one that won't go in any box, and say what it does instead.

Bring the misfit rather than the twelve. The boxes are mine, they're a tool and
not a truth, and a session where three people break them three different ways is
worth more than one where everyone's twelve fit neatly.

## Where to get the twelve

Bulbapedia's Status condition page has the non-volatile and volatile conditions
with exact effects and the generational changes marked, which is six on its own.
For the other six, pick a game you've played and read its wiki rather than your
memory. The numbers are almost never what you remember.
