## Start here, before you read the rest

Write down a four-person party from a game you know. Next to each name, finish
this sentence: "X exists because ___ can't ___."

Most people get two. The healer and the tank come out immediately. The other two
turn into "damage" and "support", which aren't roles — they're categories on a
menu.

## The definition that works

A role is a constraint one party member carries so another one doesn't have to.

Final Fantasy X is the clearest case in the genre, because it's almost rude
about it. Wakka exists because nobody else can hit a flying enemy. Lulu exists
because armoured enemies shrug off physical damage. Auron exists because
armoured enemies don't shrug off him. Tidus exists because fast enemies dodge
everyone else. Each character is one lock removed, and the game names the locks
out loud by walking enemies in front of you that exactly one party member
answers.

That design has a price, and FFX pays it in the open: swapping a character in
mid-battle is free. It has to be. If swapping cost a turn, an encounter with one
flying enemy would be unwinnable with the wrong three on the field, and the
roles would stop being roles and start being a loadout you got wrong an hour
ago. The role definition forced the swap rule. That's the direction the
causation runs, and it usually is.

## Test a role by removing it

The test is subtraction. Take the member out and see what breaks.

Remove the healer and the fight gets longer: the healer wasn't a role, they were
a damage source with a negative sign. Remove the healer and the fight becomes
unwinnable at any length: that's a constraint nobody else carries, and you've
found a real role.

Run the same test on "damage". In a lot of parties, removing the damage dealer
makes fights longer and changes nothing else. That's a dial, not a role. It
isn't a criticism — a party of four roles and no dial has no way to express a
level advantage, and week 10 is about what happens to games that can't — but you
should know which of your four are which, and most designers can't say.

## The trap

The trap is designing four roles that each remove a different constraint and
never checking that the constraints exist. A character who removes "you can't
act twice" is doing real work only in a system where acting twice matters, which
is the action economy from week 7. Roles aren't defined in the abstract. They're
defined against the specific fights you're going to run, which means you can't
finish the party before you've written an enemy.

## Make the party, then take one out

Define a four-member party where every role is named by the constraint it
removes from another role. Write the four sentences, and make them sentences
rather than labels.

Then remove one member and identify who breaks — not which fight gets harder,
but which *other role* stops working without them. If removing someone breaks
nobody, you have three roles and a passenger. Say so, and say what you'd give
them to fix it.
